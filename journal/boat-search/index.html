In Search of the Right Boat

From New Jersey to the Río Dulce — the search narrows to Teki Malo.

Some journeys begin when you cast off the dock. Mine began long before I ever stepped aboard the boat that might carry me across an ocean.

For months, I’ve been searching for something more than a sailboat. I’ve been searching for a home, a way of life, and the freedom to explore the world on my own terms.

My vision is simple: a strong, dependable bluewater sailboat capable of crossing oceans, comfortable enough to live aboard, and manageable enough to sail solo. A boat that can take me along distant coastlines, into quiet anchorages, and eventually across the open Pacific.

But finding that boat has turned into an adventure of its own.

There have been thousands of miles of research, long drives, disappointing inspections, late-night conversations with brokers, unexpected discoveries, and some difficult decisions. And now, the search is taking me to Guatemala.

Chapter 1 — The Dream Takes Shape

I’ve always been drawn to the ocean. There’s something extraordinary about being surrounded by water, relying on the wind, reading the weather, and knowing that the horizon is yours to explore.

But dreaming about sailing offshore and preparing to actually do it are two very different things.

I began studying the characteristics that separate a coastal cruiser from a true bluewater sailboat. Hull construction. Keel design. Stability. Rigging. Self-steering. Fuel and water capacity. Seaworthiness. Maintenance. The ability to survive heavy weather. And perhaps most importantly, whether a boat could be handled safely by one person.

I wasn’t looking for the biggest or flashiest yacht. I wanted a boat I could trust.

My research led me toward traditional offshore designs, particularly the legendary Tayana 37, designed by Robert Perry. The Tayana’s full keel, heavy displacement, protected rudder, and cutter rig appealed to me. It represented the kind of sailing I envisioned: long passages, remote destinations, and a boat built with the open ocean in mind.

Chapter 2 — The First Reality Checks

As summer turned toward fall, my research became more serious. Online listings were no longer enough. I needed to see these boats in person.

Three Little Birds — Cambridge, Maryland

One of my early inspections took me to Maryland to see Three Little Birds. Like many older cruising sailboats, she looked promising in photographs. But photographs don’t reveal everything.

Once aboard, I began finding the kinds of problems that can turn a dream into a long restoration project. Signs of water intrusion, deteriorated wood, questionable hatches, a neglected engine compartment, and the unmistakable smell of diesel. There were too many unanswered questions about the condition of the boat.

It was disappointing, but it taught me something important: a beautiful classic sailboat can hide decades of deferred maintenance. I wasn’t looking for a boat that would spend its first year in a boatyard. I wanted to go sailing.

Chapter 3 — Pantala: The Boat I Thought Might Be the One

Then came Pantala. A 1982 Tayana 37 in New Bern, North Carolina. Of all the boats I had researched, Pantala seemed to check many of the boxes. She had the traditional offshore design I wanted, a cutter rig, and an extensive history of work and improvements.

In October, I made the long drive from New Jersey to New Bern. Driving south through the night, I had plenty of time to imagine what might come next. If everything worked out, I could picture myself preparing the boat, sailing down the Intracoastal Waterway, finding a favorable weather window, and eventually heading offshore.

Walking aboard Pantala was exciting. Here was a boat that represented so much of what I had been looking for. But as the inspection process continued, concerns began to emerge. There were issues involving the engine, diesel odors inside the cabin, structural woodwork near the bow, and possible leaks around chainplates. The deeper we looked, the more questions appeared.

But the ocean doesn’t care how emotionally invested you are in a boat. A vessel needs to be seaworthy, not simply beautiful.

In early November, I made the decision not to proceed with Pantala. It was one of the more disappointing moments of the search. Yet I also knew it was the right decision. Sometimes the most important seamanship happens before you ever leave the dock. Knowing when to walk away may be just as valuable as knowing when to set sail.

Chapter 5 — Teki Malo: A Tayana in the Jungle

Somewhere along the Río Dulce in Guatemala sits a 1988 Tayana 37 named Teki Malo. When I first discovered her, I was intrigued. She had the familiar lines of the Tayana design I admired, but she was located in a place I had never expected my search to take me.

Guatemala. Not a typical destination for someone from New Jersey shopping for a sailboat. But Río Dulce is a fascinating place in the cruising world. The river winds inland from the Caribbean through lush tropical scenery, past limestone cliffs, jungle-covered hills, small settlements, and sheltered marinas.

I contacted Catherine Hobson of Dockside Yacht Brokerage and began gathering information. Photographs, specifications, previous survey records, equipment lists, and discussions about maintenance. I arranged a video walkthrough so I could get a better sense of the boat without immediately traveling internationally.

The search was gradually becoming something bigger than shopping for a boat. I was learning how to prepare for life in a new cruising environment.

Chapter 6 — Heading South: The Survey Trip

The decision is made. From 18 to 24 November 2026 I will be in Río Dulce for a full inspection of Teki Malo. Rigging survey, mechanical inspection, and a complete condition survey are all scheduled. This is the final filter before any commitment.

Travel Route

  • Depart Newark (preferred) or Philadelphia
  • Fly to Guatemala City (La Aurora International) — roughly 6–8 hours
  • Preferred connection: local flight Guatemala City → Puerto Barrios (new service)
  • Then a ~1.5-hour taxi into Río Dulce
  • Alternative: direct 6-hour bus (~$30) or taxi from Guatemala City

I’ll be staying at Bruno’s Hotel, a classic riverfront place with open-air dining under a pavilion that carries an anchor and ship’s wheel on the gable, a pool surrounded by palms, and docks right on the water. It sits in the heart of the cruising community that has made Río Dulce a winter base for so many boats.

The river doesn’t just hold boats. It holds the next chapter of the search.

From Bruno’s, the aerial view of the Río Dulce and Lake Izabal makes the geography clear: protected water, jungle banks, and a direct path out to the Caribbean at Livingston. The circled location on the river is where Teki Malo waits.

Chapter 7 — What Comes After

If the survey confirms what the paperwork and the video walkthrough suggest, the plan is straightforward. Base in the Río Dulce National Park / Lake Izabal area with a permit expected through roughly April. Use the protected water to finish any remaining work, learn the boat, and settle into live-aboard rhythm.

From there, the longer vision opens up:

Northbound Sketch

  • Cruise north from Livingston into Belize waters
  • Continue toward Mexico — Cancún / Yucatán coast
  • Target a mid-to-late March Gulf crossing toward Key West / Marathon, Florida
  • Overall aim of reaching Florida by May

It is still a sketch, not a schedule. Weather windows, boat readiness, and the simple reality of learning a new vessel will decide the actual timing. But the direction is clear: from the jungle river, north through the Caribbean, and eventually back to familiar waters on the other side of the Gulf.

For now, the next concrete step is the week of 18–24 November. Rigging, survey, mechanical inspection. Then a decision.
