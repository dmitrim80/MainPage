The Search for a Home That Sails

November 2026

Over the last several months I’ve been looking for a boat I can live aboard, sail mostly alone, take offshore, and still work from as a momentum day trader.

The Tayana 37 has been the center of the search, though I’ve compared it against other bluewater designs. The process has included a lot of reading, broker calls, several personal inspections, two trips to look at the same boat, a signed purchase agreement, and ultimately walking away after the survey revealed problems I wasn’t willing to take on.

What I’m actually looking for

I’m 6′2″, so usable headroom and sleeping space matter. I prefer a cutter rig, a boat under 44 feet that stays manageable, a dependable engine and rigging, and a dry interior. I can do maintenance and upgrades, but I don’t want the new home to start as a major restoration project. It also needs enough electrical capacity and physical space for a trading workstation—Starlink first, then two external 4K monitors and a reliable Windows laptop.

July–August: Research and narrowing

I spent the early months working through the practical realities of living aboard while continuing to trade. I compared Tayana 37s with Pacific Seacraft 37s, Cabo Rico 38s, Pearson 424s, and other offshore boats. By early August I was already talking about Pantala, a 1982 Tayana 37 in New Bern, North Carolina. The advertised refit made her stand out, though those were still only listing claims at that point. I also completed ASA 101 during this period.

September: Building a shortlist

Three Little Birds in Cambridge, Maryland, and Briana in Virginia became serious Tayana candidates. I also researched Teki Malo, a 1988 Tayana 37 in Río Dulce, Guatemala. Another Tayana, Deviant, was under consideration but later sold. I started paying closer attention to engine history, chainplates, standing rigging, fuel tanks, and the difference between a boat that looks equipped and one that is actually usable.

October 5: Three Little Birds

I went aboard in Cambridge and recorded photos and video. The condition was disappointing—interior rot and water damage, leaks around hatches and deck areas, a dirty or corroded engine, water in the bilge, and neglected systems. I rejected her. Three Little Birds is not an overlooked bargain; I inspected her and ruled her out.

October 6: Briana

The next day I inspected Briana at Jennings Boatyard in Reedville, Virginia. There had been promising claims about previous upgrades, but the boat itself raised concerns: diesel odor, dirt, deteriorated exterior wood, hatch issues, and uncertainty about the engine and recommissioning after years of sitting. Rigging and fuel-tank information still needed verification. Briana did not meet the goal of a dependable, near-turnkey liveaboard.

Those first two visits changed how I looked at lower asking prices. A cheap boat that requires extensive work before it can be lived on and sailed is not a bargain for the way I want to use it.

Mid-October: First visit to Pantala

Around October 15 I drove overnight from New Jersey to inspect Pantala and meet the broker. I recorded photos and video and examined the boat. She appeared to have more completed work and equipment than the previous candidates, so she became the leading option. I continued checking the reported engine work, structural repairs, rigging, electrical systems, and practical liveaboard suitability.

Late October: Agreement and planning

After the visit we negotiated and, on October 24, signed a purchase agreement subject to inspection. I began planning the practical side of living aboard—Starlink, the trading setup, equipment priorities, and possible routes south through Beaufort toward Florida or offshore toward Bermuda.

October 30: Second trip and the survey that stopped early

For the second trip I flew from Philadelphia to Raleigh/Durham, rented a car, and returned to New Bern. This visit exposed more serious problems than the first:

  • Bowsprit and forward bulkhead work were needed.
  • The engine smoked and needed proper mechanical diagnosis.
  • Diesel odor throughout the cabin was unacceptable for living aboard.
  • Chainplate leaks and rigging questions remained.
  • A satisfactory haul-out inspection was still required.

The inspection process itself was disrupted when the captain refused to move the boat to the yard. The survey stopped early, leaving important checks incomplete.

Early November: Walking away

Because of the findings I proposed a significantly lower price with conditions: resolve the fuel smell, obtain a mechanic’s diagnosis and repair estimate for the smoking engine, and complete satisfactory haul-out and rigging inspections. I was prepared to walk away.

On November 6 I formally rejected Pantala, withdrew the revised offer, and completed the release-of-escrow paperwork. A few days later the deposit was returned. The Pantala purchase was over.

Late October–early November: Expanding the search again

I continued comparing other boats, including Sans Souci, Teki Malo, additional Tayanas, Cabo Rico 38s, Island Packet 38s, a California Tayana pilothouse, and a Baba 35. Sans Souci was interesting because of reported recent offshore use, though these were still remote comparisons. I also considered the possibility of West Coast cruising and even Hawaii if the right boat appeared there, rather than automatically bringing a West Coast boat east.

November 5: Remote look at Scrimshaw

Scrimshaw is a 1979 Baba 35 in Luperón, Dominican Republic. I liked what I saw during the remote walkthrough. Headroom appeared suitable, and the broker confirmed there was no diesel smell. I requested surveys, rigging and chainplate records, engine records, deck-work documentation, and clarification of a companionway leak. I also looked into travel, sea-trial availability, ownership transfer, and closing logistics. This remained a remote evaluation; I had not yet arranged an in-person visit.

November 6–10: Teki Malo moves forward

I completed a remote walkthrough of Teki Malo and made an offer subject to satisfactory inspections. Purchase-agreement revisions and an addendum were exchanged. I contacted a surveyor about a pre-purchase survey, haul-out, and sea trial, with a travel window of November 16–30. As of November 10 that inspection trip was still ahead of me—I had not yet been to Guatemala. I also began researching Río Dulce charts, travel, provisioning, and routes toward Florida.

Where the search stands

I have personally inspected three different Tayanas, with two separate trips to Pantala—four in-person boat visits in total. I rejected Three Little Birds and Briana after seeing their condition, and I went further with Pantala before inspection findings caused me to cancel the purchase and recover the deposit.

Teki Malo and Scrimshaw became the next serious prospects. Active purchase paperwork and inspection planning are underway for Teki Malo, but nothing has closed.

The clearest lesson so far is that a respected bluewater design, an attractive equipment list, or a claimed refit cannot substitute for a dry, livable interior, sound structure, dependable machinery, and verified maintenance. I need a boat whose actual condition supports the way I intend to live and sail.
